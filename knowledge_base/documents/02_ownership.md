# Shares, investors, loans and business cash

## What is a share and who is a shareholder?

A share is a small unit of ownership in a company. Someone owning shares is a
shareholder. Owning a share is not the same as owning a fixed amount of cash in
the company's bank account. A shareholder may receive dividends and may gain or
lose money as the market price changes.

When someone buys an existing listed share, the payment normally goes to its
previous owner, not to the company. A new share issue can raise fresh money for
the company. Do not describe every stock-market purchase as directly financing
the illustrative expansion.

## How can shareholders earn money? Must a good expansion raise the share price?

Shareholders can receive dividends, if declared, and can sell shares for more than
they paid, if the price rises. Neither is guaranteed. Prices respond to expected
future cash, risk, interest rates and information already reflected in the market.
An expansion reporting positive cash flow does not mechanically guarantee a
higher share price: investors may have expected even better results.

## How are shareholders different from lenders?

Shareholders provide ownership capital and bear the residual business risk.
Lenders provide borrowed money under a contract and expect repayment and interest.
Lenders generally have priority over shareholders, but loans can still default.
Interest is often less risky than equity returns, not universally “small” or
guaranteed. A loan is not the same as buying shares in the company.

## Why borrow if the company already has money?

A company may preserve an operating cash buffer, spread financing across sources,
or match borrowing to a specific investment. Having some cash does not mean every
rupee is available for a new project. Borrowing also creates interest and repayment
obligations; it is not automatically beneficial. This case does not establish that
Infosys took a particular loan for the illustrative expansion.

## Is almost 100% equity funding the same as all cash being shareholders' money?

No. A market-value equity weight used in WACC is a financing-value ratio, not a
breakdown of the bank balance. Company cash can reflect customer collections,
retained earnings, borrowing, new equity and other transactions. A business can
have little recorded debt without all current cash having been freshly deposited
by shareholders. Market capitalisation is not the company's available cash.

## What is an expansion and who generates the later cash flows?

An expansion means spending now to add business capacity or operations, such as
new facilities, delivery capability or services. In this hypothetical case, the
expanded business generates later cash through its operations. Customers pay the
business; costs and investment needs use some of that cash; the remaining assumed
free cash is used in the valuation. Shareholders are not assumed to deposit the
year-one, year-two and year-three cash flows themselves.

## Are free cash flow, revenue, profit and dividends the same?

No. Revenue records sales. Accounting profit follows accounting recognition and
expense rules. Free cash flow is cash remaining after the operating costs, cash
taxes and investment needs included in the model. A dividend is cash the company
chooses to distribute to shareholders. This expansion uses unlevered project cash
before financing payments; it does not assume every rupee is paid as a dividend.
